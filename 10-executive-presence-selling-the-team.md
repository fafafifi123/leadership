# Pillar 10 — Executive Presence & Selling the Equities Tech Team
## Shifting from Silent Executor to Aggressive Internal Champion

**Layer:** Business | **Great Leader Anchor:** Jamie Dimon (JPMorgan Chase) | **Related:** [Pillar 05](./05-managing-up-influence-negotiation.md) · [Pillar 09](./09-effective-technical-communication.md)

**Hogan Filter — Your Caution Trap:** This is your primary 360-degree feedback gap. You let stable code and zero outages speak for themselves — in silence. Your MD and trading desk head do not experience your team's value; they experience the absence of failures. Invisible reliability is not a competitive asset. It is a budget-cut target.

**Anti-Caution Directive:** Your team's value is not self-evident to anyone who doesn't sit in your sprint reviews. You are the only one who can translate engineering output into business language. If you don't do it, it doesn't get done.

## In 60 Seconds

In equities technology, failures are always visible — P1 incidents page everyone. Successes are structurally invisible unless you make them visible. The equation your business stakeholders run unconsciously: **Perceived Team Value = (Visible Wins × Frequency) − (Visible Failures × Severity)**. Your current operating mode: minimize failures, never file the wins. Their perception: "Technology is stable. Do we need 5 engineers for that?"

## Dimon's Sponsorship Model

Dimon's operating belief: **"The moment your business line forgets that technology is enabling their revenue, you have lost the political ground to ask for investment, headcount, or organizational priority."**

His technology leaders are expected to:
1. **Narrate every major delivery** in commercial terms at the business review
2. **Attach a dollar or risk value** to every significant uptime improvement or latency win
3. **Show up at the desk** — not just in incident bridges, but in normal trading hours asking what isn't working
4. **Build personal relationships** with 2–3 trading desk heads, not just their IT sponsor

## The Executive Presence Operating System

### Layer 1 — Presence in Communications (Weekly)

**The 3-Win Weekly Dispatch** — send every Friday:
```
Equities Tech — Week of [Date]

✓ Shipped: [Delivery 1] — [one-sentence business outcome]
✓ Shipped: [Delivery 2] — [one-sentence business outcome]
✓ Hardened: [System improvement] — [risk or cost avoided]
↗ Next week: [What's coming that they should know about]
⚠ Watch item: [One risk on the horizon — optional but powerful]
```

10 minutes to write. Compound interest on your visibility account.

### Layer 2 — Presence in Meetings (Always On)

**The Assertive Chair Protocol:**
When in a business review, roadmap session, or technology steering meeting:
- **Do not wait to be asked.** Proactively surface your team's last 30-day impact in the first 5 minutes.
- **Quantify before you qualify.** Lead with numbers: "My team shipped 6 features this quarter, maintained 99.96% uptime during earnings season, and reduced end-of-day reporting latency by 62%."
- **Name your engineers.** "This was driven primarily by [Engineer Name]'s redesign of the position aggregation service." Individual attribution raises team visibility and signals strong management.

### Layer 3 — Presence with the Business (Proactive)

**The Desk Walk Protocol** — once per week, 15 minutes:
Spend time on the trading floor or in a video call with a trading desk stakeholder — not during an incident, not to discuss a ticket. To ask one question: **"What is the most friction-generating technology experience you had this week?"**

This does three things:
1. Surfaces real UX problems your team would never see in a JIRA backlog
2. Builds relationship capital you will need during the next incident
3. Generates the commercial language you need for your managing-up communications

## Selling the Team — Scripts

### Script 1 — Business Review Opening
```
"Before I get into the roadmap, I want to take 2 minutes to close the loop
on last quarter. My team shipped [N] production features, maintained [uptime%]
across [key systems], and delivered [specific metric improvement] that directly
impacted [specific business function].

The metric I'm most proud of: [single most commercially relevant stat].
That's the foundation I'm building next quarter's roadmap on."
```

### Script 2 — Responding to "What Does Your Team Actually Do?"
```
"Three things. First, we keep [system] running at [uptime%] — that's the
infrastructure every position on the desk flows through. Second, we ship
[N features/quarter] that make the desk faster at [specific workflow].
Third, we prevent the technology failures that would cause [specific risk outcome].

The investment in my team is the difference between [business-as-usual outcome]
and [failure scenario]. I can give you the numbers if it would be useful."
```

### Script 3 — Advocating for Headcount or Budget
```
"I'm going to make a commercial case, not a technical one.

Current state: [N engineers] covering [X systems, Y trading desks, Z critical
workflows]. Constraint: [specific capability gap — e.g., no dedicated
performance engineering, single point of failure in on-call coverage].

Business cost of constraint: [X incidents/year, Y hours manual ops, Z latency
impact on fills].

Investment ask: [1 engineer / $X tooling budget / Y additional capacity].
Expected return: [40% reduction in P2 incidents, fill-rate improvement,
ops cost avoidance of ~$X/year].

I can model this in more detail if you want the numbers."
```

## The Caution Trap in Executive Presence

Your Hogan profile will push you toward:
- **Assuming the work speaks for itself** — it doesn't, in a bank or on a trading floor
- **Framing visibility as self-promotion** — it isn't; it is stakeholder management and team advocacy
- **Waiting for the quarterly business review** — by then budget decisions have been made in informal conversations you weren't part of
- **Under-attributing individual engineers** to avoid seeming like you play favorites

**The Dimon Override:** Your team's value is not self-evident to anyone who doesn't sit in your sprint reviews. You are the only translator. If you don't do it, it doesn't get done — and your team pays for it in budget cuts and headcount freezes.
