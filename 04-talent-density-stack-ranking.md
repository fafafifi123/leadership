# Pillar 04 — Talent Density & Stack Ranking
## High-Integrity Performance Management for a 5-Person Equities Tech Squad

**Great Leader Anchor:** Steve Jobs (Apple)
**Hogan Filter:** Your caution default is to avoid differentiation — treating all five engineers as "solid contributors" to reduce the discomfort of hard ranking conversations. This creates a low-density team where your best engineer is carrying the weight of your weakest without recognition or consequence for either.

---

## The Core Framework: Quantitative Performance Matrix

For a 5-person team, stack ranking must be based on observable, multi-dimensional data — not gut feel or tenure.

```
DIMENSION 1 — Technical Output (40%)
  - Story points delivered vs. committed (sprint accuracy)
  - PR merge rate and code review cycle time
  - Production defect escape rate (bugs shipped to prod)
  - System complexity handled (P1 systems vs. low-criticality)

DIMENSION 2 — Operational Reliability (30%)
  - On-call incident response time
  - MTTR on incidents they owned
  - Unplanned outages caused or escalated by their code

DIMENSION 3 — Team Leverage (20%)
  - Knowledge transfer (documentation, pair sessions)
  - Unblocking others (cross-PR reviews, architectural guidance)
  - Mentoring junior engineers

DIMENSION 4 — Commercial Awareness (10%)
  - Proactively engages trading desk or business stakeholders
  - Understands how their system touches PnL or risk workflows
```

---

## Steve Jobs' Talent Density Principle

Jobs ran Apple on one immovable belief: **"One A-player is worth three B-players. The cost of carrying a C-player is not their salary — it is the A-player you fail to retain because you tolerate mediocrity around them."**

For your 5-person team, this translates directly:
- You can have **2–3 A-players** who are fully stretched and compensated accordingly
- You can carry **1–2 B-players** who are on a defined growth trajectory
- You cannot carry a **C-player** who is not on a documented improvement path with a decision date

A C-player in equities tech is not just an output problem. They are a **systemic risk** — their on-call gaps, their code defects, and their architectural blindspots become your P1 incidents.

Rank 5 should trigger an immediate conversation — not a mental note.

## Performance Intervention Scripts

### Script 1 — A-Player Recognition & Retention
```
"I want to be direct with you: you are the highest-impact engineer on this team.
In [specific quarter], you shipped [X deliveries], kept [system] at [uptime],
and were the reason [business outcome] landed on time.

I'm going to make sure that's reflected in your comp review. More importantly,
I want to understand what your next 18-month technical challenge looks like —
because I don't want to lose you to a problem I could have given you here."
```

### Script 2 — B-Player Growth Contract
```
"You're delivering consistently but there's a ceiling forming. The gap I see is
[specific dimension — e.g., operational ownership, cross-team leverage].

Here's my offer: I'll assign you lead ownership of [specific system or project]
for next quarter. That's intentional stretch. In exchange, I need [specific
behavioral target — e.g., PR review cycle time under 1 day, zero on-call
escalations]. We review this at the 8-week mark."
```

### Script 3 — C-Player PIP Initiation
```
"I'm going to be direct because I respect you enough not to let this drift.
Over the last [N weeks], [specific pattern: e.g., 3 production defects,
2 missed sprint commitments, 1 escalated P2 incident].

This is not a surprise conversation — I flagged [earlier instance] in our 1:1
on [date]. We are now at the point where I need to put this on a formal
improvement plan. Here's what that means: [specific measurable targets],
[timeline], [support available], and [consequence if not met].

I want you to succeed here. But I need a different output trajectory
starting this sprint."
```

## Safe PIP / Exit Execution — 5-Person Team Considerations

With only 5 engineers, a PIP or exit has **immediate operational impact**. Sequence matters:

1. **Before PIP initiation:** Ensure knowledge transfer plan exists for their systems
2. **During PIP period:** Do not assign new critical-path work — stabilize their current ownership
3. **Exit decision:** Pre-brief your MD and HR before the conversation, not after
4. **Post-exit:** Redistribute on-call load temporarily; do not silently overload your A-players
5. **Backfill:** Start the hiring process at PIP initiation, not at exit decision

---

## The Caution Trap in Talent Management

Your Hogan profile will push you toward:
- **Rating inflation:** Everyone gets "meets expectations" to avoid conflict
- **Delayed PIP:** Waiting for a fourth or fifth event before formalizing
- **Protecting the weak:** Shielding underperformers from high-stakes assignments that would reveal the gap
- **Confusing effort with output:** Rewarding visible effort rather than measurable impact

**The Jobs Override:** Ask yourself quarterly — "If this engineer resigned tomorrow, would I fight to keep them?" If the answer is no for any of your 5, you already know what action is required.

## Weekly Experiments

1. **This week:** Score each of your 5 engineers privately across the 4 dimensions above. Identify your rank 5. Write down the one conversation you have been avoiding with them. Schedule it.
2. **This week:** Have a 15-minute "retention" conversation with your rank 1 engineer. Ask them: "What technical problem are you not getting to work on that you wish you were?" Then solve it.
